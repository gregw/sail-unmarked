Project brief · for Claude Code

Unmarkable Racing

Sail racing around virtual marks.

An app for sailing races around virtual marks, where every mark is a line to be crossed via GPS rather than a point to be rounded. The intention of the application is both to allow more flexible traditional racing without the hassle of laying marks; and to support some new racing formats made possible by virtual marks and app tracking.

Scope for this document

This document is a specification to read and plan from. The task is to understand the model, raise questions, and propose an architecture and a build order. Until and implementation is developed, everything in this document is provisional.

Revised after the first build. Sections 2, 5, 6, 7 and 8 have been brought into line with what was actually decided and implemented. The changes of substance: line ends are now named port and starboard; the crossing test is stated as two separate tests, sense and extent; the system has one declared resolution of one metre; and the configuration sketch in section 6 has been replaced outright — the original was wrong in specific ways, listed there so they are not reintroduced. The working notes live in CLAUDE.md, which is now the more current of the two on anything to do with the schema.

01Introduction

The core idea is to replace the rounding of a physical mark (checked with the eyeball) with the crossing of a virtual line (checked by the app). A line is used rather than a point mark to avoid the need for Racing Rule of Sailing 18 (RRS18 - Mark Roundings) and to limit GPS accuracy issues that may create disputes over whether a boat clipped a rounding radius or not. Already, fleets routinely cross the start lines without the benefit of RRS18 and often rely on GPS instruments to site the line as either one or both ends of the line cannot be seen. This application extends that style of crossing to every mark rounding.

RRS 18 (mark-room) never applies at a virtual mark. There is no zone to reach and no inside or outside overlap to adjudicate. That is a deliberate safety/simplicity choice, not a side effect, and it shapes both the rules framing in section 4 and the crossing logic in section 2.

The system has two parts. A client on the boat that reads GPS, detects crossings, times them, and stores its own race record, working with no network. A server on shore that distributes course and format definitions and aggregates results. The dividing principle, stated once and relied on throughout: a boat's own rounding and timing are computed entirely on the device and never wait on a server.

02Virtual marks: the core model

Lines, not points

A virtual mark is a line. A crossing is valid when the boat passes from the required side to the opposite side in the required direction. There is no proximity radius and no single point of contest.

Lines are defined by two points, as current start/finish lines are today. However, either or both ends may be defined to be infinite, so a line may not be geographically limited if that suites the course design. Furthermore, either/both ends of a line may be placed on shore, to avoid issues with near-end crossings. These lines can be used to mimic traditional marks and course infrastructure:

  • Start/Finish Line: A finite virtual line can exactly represent a traditional start/finish line marked by a boat and pin. There is obviously no visual indication on the water of the line end, but boats are often unsighted to the line ends anyway. The application will provide easy to understand visualisations to help a boat cross a line, similar to start sequence instrument displays that are already used. In some circumstances, partial or fully infinite lines can be used to replace lines defined by shore infrastructure and a bearing.
  • Rounding Mark:A virtual line can be used to replace a rounding mark. Boats much approach that line and get a positive indication from the application of a crossing before changing course onto the next leg. If the rounding is likely to be contested, then a finite line perpendicular to the approaching leg can be used and will be crossed by the fleet similar to a starting line is today. A lightly contested rounding (e.g. pursuit race) can be modelled with a half infinite line, starting at a point where a traditional mark should be and extending infinitely away in the direction of the approaching leg.
  • Passing Mark: A virtual line parallel to the leg direction, but placed off to one side or the other of the course, can be used to mimic a passing mark.
  • Gate Mark: A pair of virtual lines, parallel to the leg direction, placed either side of the gate location will mimic a gate mark by offering boats a choice of which to cross.
  • Island Rounding: An island or landmark can be rounded by placing one or more half-infinite line(s) with non-infinite end(s) on the shore.

The ends are named port and starboard

A line's two ends are called port and starboard, and those names are what orient it:

  • Forward — leave the port end to port and the starboard end to starboard.
  • Reverse — leave the port end to starboard and the starboard end to port.

The required sense is then legible off the line itself and there is no convention to memorise. Note that the names are defined relative to a forward crossing rather than being a property of the line sitting on its own, so a line crossed both ways — the leeward line that is start, mark 2 and finish — is forward once and reverse twice.

Sense and extent are two different tests

Deciding a crossing is two independent questions, and keeping them apart is what makes infinite ends behave.

TestQuestionInvolves where the ends are?
SenseWhich way did the boat cross?No. A sign test on the port→starboard orientation.
ExtentDid it cross the line, or its extension out past a finite end?Only finite ends.

An infinite end is a bearing, not a place. The lat/long given for it is a point the line runs through, not where it stops. So the rule needs no exception at infinity: a forward crossing leaves the port end to port and the starboard end to starboard; an infinite end is out along the line without limit, so it is always on the side its name says — and therefore only a finite end can be missed.

"Leave the starboard end to starboard" is a mnemonic for the sense test written in the vocabulary of the extent test, which is why it sounds paradoxical at infinity: the point that defined an infinite end can easily fall on the other hand from the end itself. The point was never the end.

The Mark screen's three states in section 3 are exactly the combinations. Approaching is neither settled; crossed is both passed; missed is the sense test passed and the extent test failed.

05Course definition

Courses are built from the same lines as everything else. Each line is two points, each with an infinite flag, and a course is an ordered sequence of crossings over them. A crossing is a required sense, drawn as a triangle whose base sits on the line and whose apex points the way you must cross, carrying its sequence letter inside. One line can hold several triangles, so a single line can be the start, a rounding, and the finish at once.

Lines are defined once and shared between courses, not owned by one. A club's start line is the same line in every course it appears in, and moving it should be one edit. The word is line rather than mark for the same reason it is throughout: courses may eventually mix real marks with virtual lines, and the two need different words.

The application will have course visualisation screens that can show either abstract courses like the ones below, or the actual virtual lines laid over a marine chart.

Forward means you leave the port end to port and the starboard end to starboard; reverse is the opposite. A crossing the wrong way is logged but ignored, so a rounding is a single crossing in the required sense, never an up-and-back.

Roles and letters are positional. There is no role: start field. The first step of a course is the start and the last is the finish, and the sequence letters are derived from position for the same reason — a stored letter can disagree with the order it sits in. The two-lap windward/leeward below gets S, 2 and F onto one line for free, because that line simply appears three times in the sequence. A consequence: neither the first nor the last step may be a gate, and once real marks exist, neither may be a mark. You cannot start or finish on a point.

A gate is a step that offers alternatives, each of which is an ordinary step, so the two sides need not be the same kind of thing — a gate with a virtual line one side and a real buoy the other is just a list with one of each in it. The leg into a gate is measured to the midpoint between its alternatives, so a course length does not depend on which side a given boat took.

Leg length, and the handle

A leg is measured between the midpoints of consecutive lines' two defined points, and a course length is the sum of its legs. A measured leg of zero is an error rather than a short leg: it means two steps share a reference point, which no course means. A step may override the leg into it outright, for a length that is a matter of record rather than of geometry.

The subtle part is deliberate. On an infinite end the point's distance along the bearing is arbitrary — sliding it leaves the line geometrically identical — so that point is the control handle for where the line is measured to. Place it where the fleet actually crosses. A course-design UI draws the leg straight to the midpoint and lets somebody drag the handle until the course looks like the one they meant. The same handle is what the distance-factor format in section 4 turns.

Two-lap windward / leeward

The base case. A start / leeward / finish line carrying start up, mark 2 down and finish down; a windward line carrying 1 and 3 up. Read the leeward line in course order and its triangles are forward, reverse, reverse.

Triangle, sausage, sausage

Start up (S), round the windward gate (1), reach out to the wing and back (2, first lap only), down through the start line (3), then two sausages up and down the windward gate: 4 up, 5 down, 6 up, finish down (F).

Triple sausage, detached gates

Three beats to the windward gate (1, 3, 5) with two downwind gate roundings (2, 4). Here the gates are detached: rather than hanging off the ends of the start line, each is its own pair of half-infinite lines set below and to the sides, rounded either side.

Crossing detection

Detect a crossing as the intersection of the segment between two successive quality fixes with the line, not as proximity to the line. Proximity misreads a fast crossing of a thin line and cannot give a clean crossing instant.

Fix quality control

A single out-of-position fix is more dangerous to a line test than to almost anything else. If a flyer lands on the far side, the segment out and the segment back both cut the line, manufacturing a matched pair of crossings the boat never made. Fixes must be quality controlled. Layer the controls:

  1. Receiver metadata pre-filter: reject on fix type, low satellite count, or a stated accuracy worse than a generous threshold. Necessary but not sufficient, since a wrong fix sometimes still reports optimistic accuracy.
  2. Kinematic plausibility gate (primary defence): from the last known-good position and time, reject any fix whose implied ground speed is physically impossible for a boat. There is large headroom between real boat motion and a genuine flyer, so this gate has almost no false positives and catches the gross outliers that cause false crossings.
  3. Confirmed side change: do not let any single segment commit a rounding. Catches the moderate outlier that passes the kinematic gate.

Prefer clean rejection and interpolation across good fixes over a heavy smoother. A filter aggressive enough to reject outliers also lags true position and biases the crossing instant, which is the one quantity the score depends on.

Confirmation and timing

These are two separate jobs and must stay separate.

  • Validity requires at least N consecutive quality fixes confirming the required side, a segment that intersects the line, then at least N consecutive quality fixes confirming the opposite side. Default N is 3, giving a 3-and-3 rule. A single flyer cannot produce 3 consecutive confirmed fixes on the far side, so it fails. N is a tunable default, not a fixed truth. See the open questions.
  • Time is the interpolated instant at which the segment cuts the line. It is never the time of any fix. Taking the time from a confirming fix biases it, early if from the last near-side fix, late if from the first far-side fix.

Direction, ducks, and latching

Trigger only on a confirmed crossing in the required direction. Log wrong-direction crossings for audit but do not score them. Once a required-direction crossing is confirmed it latches and stands, so nothing later can un-make it. This is monotone and needs no running parity that a late bad fix could corrupt. The taut-string winding test used for point marks is not used, because it is undefined for a line: a line holds no loops.

Accuracy band

A boat is resolved to a side only when it is clear of the line by more than the fix uncertainty. Close to the line it is not yet on either side, which stops a boat sitting on the line from generating a burst of phantom crossings. Whether the band width should be a fixed declared value or the per-fix stated accuracy is an open choice, noted in section 8. Both are implemented; a null declared width selects the per-fix behaviour.

Note that this band belongs to the sense test — it is about the line, not about the ends. There is deliberately no equivalent band at an endpoint; see below.

One metre, and a hard edge

The resolution of the whole system is one metre. Every distance computed, displayed or scored against is rounded to the nearest metre, and the figure is declared once on each side of the client/server split so the two cannot drift. GNSS on a phone does not honestly resolve better than this, and a scoring edge that moves with the twelfth decimal place of a float is one nobody can argue in front of a protest committee.

The consequence is deliberate and blunt: a finite end is a hard edge. If you miss, you miss. There is no unresolved state at an endpoint and no band of doubt to fall into. This settles what was previously listed as an open question about endpoints in open water.

That rule is only defensible if the boat was told, so what the application owes the sailor instead is warning. The Mark screen projects the boat's COG onto the line, reports the margin in metres from that projected cut to the nearer finite end, and says loudly when the present course is running out past it — long before the boat gets there. An infinite end raises no warning, because it is not a hazard.

Audit trail

Log raw fixes, the QC decision for each, and every crossing candidate including rejected ones. A contested result then has a defensible record, for example "fix at t rejected, implied ground speed 63 kn". This matters more here than in most apps, because the app is scoring people.

Live place, circuit format

The second screen answers a different question: where you stand in the fleet right now, on handicap, when boats started at different times, carry different handicaps, and are on different parts of the course. It is the live view behind the circuit format's rankings. The idea is to place every boat at the moment its handicap-adjusted time equals yours, and compare how far each has got.

There are two modes, and the mode decides where the handicap is applied so it is never applied twice. In a same-course race every boat sails the same distance and the handicap sits in the time, so the reference clock is your corrected elapsed time (elapsed by your TCF) and boats are compared by distance to finish at that corrected time. In a corrected-distance race the handicap sits in each boat's course length instead, first home on actual elapsed wins, so the same picture is used with the reference clock set to your actual elapsed time and no TCF applied. Two teaching cases in the mock

Bombora sits behind you on the water, its current dot is left of your line, yet its projection reaches past you, so on corrected time it is ahead. Currawong is the mirror, physically ahead with its current dot past your line, but its position at your corrected time is behind you. Current position and corrected standing are different questions, which is why the screen carries both the boat head and the current dot.

04Race formats

Each format uses the virtual-mark machinery to dissolve the mass start and the single rounding point, which is where contact risk concentrates.

Self-timed rolling start

Each boat starts in its own time within a window. Its clock starts when it crosses the start line, timestamped by the device. This removes the start-line scrum and needs no committee-boat sightline.

Distance-factor handicap

Convert each boat's TCF to a distance factor, then set that boat an individual virtual mark so its course length is adjusted. First home, or fastest elapsed, wins. Handicap becomes visible and raceable on the water rather than a results-sheet calculation after the finish.

The mechanism, now settled. A course designates certain lines as handicap-adjustable, and each boat is assigned its own sub-line on one. Typically a half-infinite line running along the leg direction from a finite end, so that the finite end is the knob: the line only exists from that end outward, so pushing it further out forces the boat to sail further before there is any line there to cross. The server slides that line's two defined points along the line until the resulting midpoint makes the leg before plus the leg after come to that boat's required distance. Where a course designates several such lines, the handicap distance is shared between them.

This reuses machinery the model already has rather than adding any. The midpoint of a line's two points is already how a leg is measured, and the point given for an infinite end already changes no geometry — so it was already free to be used as a handle, and the handicap is simply what turns it. Note where this puts the server: it is now in the geometry, computing each boat's course before the start, and still not in the rounding. A boat caches its own course and detects and times its own crossings from raw GNSS exactly as before.

The conversion from a TCF to a length delta interacts with leg geometry and wind angle, and remains open — see section 8.

Circuit, join anywhere

Boats join a defined loop at any point, finish where they entered, and are ranked live. Because the loop is location-agnostic, boats from different clubs can each sail the same circuit from their own home water, on their own evening window, without travelling to a common start. This is aimed squarely at participation decline and calendar congestion. Live rankings are network-dependent and degrade gracefully to last-known standings. A boat's own record never depends on the network.

Rules framing: RRS as the default

The default is the Racing Rules of Sailing, with one exclusion: RRS 18 (mark-room) never applies at a virtual mark. There is no point to touch and no inside or outside overlap to adjudicate, so boats meeting near a line fall back on the Part 2 right-of-way rules alone. Spreading the fleet in time and space through the formats above already leaves those rules little to govern.

COLREGS could be used instead, and is the more honest choice for the asynchronous and cross-club formats. RRS Part 2 displaces the collision regulations only as a mutual agreement among competitors who have collectively accepted it, and boats from different clubs, on their own time windows, often never in sight of each other, have made no such agreement. Where that mutual basis is absent, COLREGS is the rule set with real standing between two boats that happen to meet while independently sailing the same loop. This is a per-event choice to be stated in the sailing instructions, not something the app decides.

What the earlier sketch got wrong

Recorded so it is not reintroduced. The first version of this section proposed a different shape, and each of these was a defect rather than a matter of taste.

type: finite | half_infinite contradicted the model. The end type belongs to each end, and hoisting it to the line forced two incompatible schemas — end_a/end_b for one kind of line, fixed_end plus bearing_deg for the other — behind a type key that was derivable from the ends anyway.

cross: S collided with itself. It meant "southbound", while S is also the sequence letter for the start: two meanings for one letter, two lines apart in the same file, with cross: N on the Obelisk line one keystroke from being read as a sequence letter too.

Compass senses are not safe to store. Ambiguous for a line lying near the direction of travel, and silently wrong the moment somebody edits the line. Forward/reverse moves with the line.

marks: re-imported the word the whole design exists to remove, and leaves nothing to call a real mark when mixed courses arrive.

TBD in a numeric field is a parse error, not a placeholder. An unsupplied position is null, and is reported as a problem by name.

snake_case is not the house style. sail-jinx and sailing-pf are camelCase throughout, and matching them matters more than which is nicer.

Races are their own files

A programme file holds what is reusable all season: the surveyed points, the lines, the courses. A race is one night — a course chosen from that programme, the format, that format's parameters, and the entry list — and lives in its own file at clubs/<club>/races/<raceId>.yaml. Filing the two together would mean opening the file that holds the coordinates every time somebody enters a boat.

The race schema is still open. What each format actually needs is unsettled, so its parameters are carried as a free map that round-trips, rather than a schema asserted ahead of the requirement.

07Technology

Client, on the boat

The UI is HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The wrapper decides how that same build reaches a phone versus a browser, and whether it can log GPS while backgrounded, which is the deciding constraint.

  • Wrapper: Capacitor. One web codebase wraps as native Android and iOS, where a background-geolocation plugin gives continuous tracking with the screen off. The same build without the native shell is the browser fallback, so the fallback is the same product, not a second one.
  • Offline-first, non-negotiable. The course and format definitions are downloaded and cached before the start. Crossing detection, fix QC, and latching all run on-device from raw GNSS, which needs no network. GNSS also provides a precise time source worth using in preference to the system clock.
  • Network is opportunistic only. Results sync when signal returns, and live circuit rankings degrade to last-known standings. The boat's own rounding and timing never wait on a server.
Verify at build time

Background-geolocation behaviour, iOS Safari suspension of the Geolocation API in the fallback, and Capacitor and plugin version details all shift over time. Treat the direction of these constraints as sound and confirm the current specifics when building rather than taking them as settled here.

Server, on shore

Java 21 on an embedded Jetty 12 server, YAML configuration, and JSON file persistence, following the same approach as the sail-jinx and sailing-pf projects. Maven, Jackson, no database and no framework. Port 8083, since 8081 and 8082 are those two projects and 8888 is sailing-pf's admin connector. Initial deployment on a Raspberry Pi, served at unmarkable.mortbay.org (proposed).

  • Serve course, format, and handicap definitions to clients.
  • Receive and store race records as JSON files.
  • Aggregate results and compute live circuit rankings.
  • Optionally compute distance factors from Performance Factors, reusing the sailing-pf read side.

The server is explicitly outside the rounding path. It distributes and aggregates, it does not adjudicate a crossing. The distance-factor format is the one place it computes geometry — each boat's sub-line, before the start — and even there the boat still detects and times its own crossings from raw GNSS.

Positions are SignalK's

latitude and longitude, spelled out, signed decimal degrees, WGS84. This is not a preference: the SignalK specification's schemas/definitions.json documents the deg unit as "latitude or longitude in decimal degrees" and defines navigation.position as {latitude, longitude, altitude}. Matching it means a position off a SignalK stream drops in with no conversion, and a conversion is where a hemisphere gets inverted.

Degrees and decimal minutes — 33 48.072 S — is what a chart and a plotter show, so it belongs on screen, in both directions, where a bad transcription can be caught by the person who made it. It does not belong in a file, where the same mistake is a silently displaced mark.

Conventions taken from the repositories

The sail-jinx and sailing-pf repositories have now been read, and the conventions here are theirs rather than assertions: records for models with compact constructors for defaults, Allman braces, defensive loading that reports and skips rather than throwing, unknown YAML and JSON properties ignored so a newer file loads on an older build, clubs keyed by domain, atomic writes with an append-only journal, and a StaticResourceServlet that resolves <!-- INCLUDE --> without a templating engine.

One convention is worth naming because it decides where the important code goes. sail-jinx keeps its scoring in static/scoring.js with scoring-test.html as an executable specification run from the Maven build. The same split applies here for the same reason: the logic that decides a result runs on the boat, so it is JavaScript, and its specification runs in the build or it rots.

Data flow

  1. Client fetches course and format config from the server before the start and caches it.
  2. Client logs raw fixes, QC decisions, and crossings locally throughout the race.
  3. Client posts its race record to the server when signal allows; the server stores it as JSON.
  4. Server aggregates records and serves standings; the circuit format polls for live rankings when connected.

08Open questions

Two of the original seven have been answered by the first build and are struck through with what was decided. The rest stand, and none of them should be answered by asserting a number in code.

  • Confirmation count N. The 3-and-3 default should be tuned against real logged tracks from the intended waters, where multipath off the rig and off nearby structures clusters. Sustained multipath could in principle produce consecutive bad fixes, which argues for either a higher count or a harder kinematic gate.
  • Accuracy band. Fixed declared width versus per-fix stated accuracy. The per-fix option is more honest but gives a time-varying effective line width. Both are implemented and the choice is a configuration key; which is right is undecided without logged accuracy figures from the actual devices.
  • Finite line endpoints in open water. Answered. One-metre resolution throughout, and the end is a hard edge: if you miss, you miss. The obligation moves to the interface, which must warn on the COG projection running near or past a finite end well before the boat gets there. See section 2. What remains is building the screen that does the warning.
  • Asynchronous window width. How far apart in time boats can sail the same loop before differing wind and tide mean they are no longer racing the same course.
  • Distance-factor conversion. The mechanism is settled — the server slides a designated line's points along itself until the midpoint gives the required leg lengths, with the finite end as the knob. The conversion from a TCF to that length delta is not, and interacts with leg geometry and wind angle. Also open: how the distance is shared when a course designates several adjustable lines.
  • QC thresholds as configuration. Kinematic ceiling, minimum satellites, accuracy limit. All are configuration keys already; all are currently defaults asserted against no data.
  • Datum. Answered: WGS84, signed decimal degrees, SignalK field names. Coordinate supply is still open — how marks get surveyed and by whom. Every position in the repository is null today.
  • Race and format schema. What each format actually needs: how a rolling-start window is expressed, how wide an asynchronous circuit window may be, where a boat's TCF comes from. Carried as a free map until there is a requirement to shape it.
  • Authentication on record submission. There is none. Anything can post a record for any boat. Reads should stay open — a club publishes its results — but a write is somebody's race result. sail-jinx has a working Jetty OpenID setup to copy.
  • Capacitor. Not yet present. Background-geolocation behaviour, iOS Safari suspending the Geolocation API in the browser fallback, and plugin versions all shift; confirm at build time rather than from section 7.
Provenance and uncertainty.
· The line model, formats, QC approach, and rules framing are settled from the design discussion and stated with confidence.
· The port/starboard end naming, the sense/extent split, the one-metre resolution, the positional roles, the leg-measurement rule and the file layout are settled from the design discussion that followed the first build, and are implemented.
· The configuration in section 6 replaces an earlier sketch that was withdrawn. What was wrong with it is recorded beside it deliberately.
· Required crossing senses in the worked course are inferred, not stated, and must be set and confirmed in config.
· No coordinates are asserted anywhere; every position is null until a survey is supplied.
· The client technology recommendation is firm in direction; version-specific and OS-specific claims are still to be verified at build time, and Capacitor is not yet present.
· The server conventions are now taken from the sail-jinx and sailing-pf repositories, which have been read, rather than assumed.
· The coordinate format is taken from the SignalK specification, which has been read.
· On-screen values are illustrative placeholders to show layout, not computed positions.
